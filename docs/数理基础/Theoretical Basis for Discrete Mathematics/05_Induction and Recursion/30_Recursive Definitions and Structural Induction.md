# 30_Recursive Definitions and Structural Induction

## 1. Recursively Defined Functions

A recursive (or inductive) definition of a function $f(n)$ over non-negative integers consists of two steps:

1. **Basis Step:** Specify the value of the function at zero (or another initial starting point).

2. **Recursive Step:** Provide a rule for finding its value at an integer based on its values at smaller integers.

_Note: A function $f(n)$ defined this way is equivalent to a sequence $a_0, a_1, \dots$ where $f(i) = a_i$._

**Examples of Recursive Functions:**

- **Arbitrary Function:** Given $f(0) = 3$ and $f(n+1) = 2f(n) + 3$.

    - $f(1) = 2(3) + 3 = 9$

    - $f(2) = 2(9) + 3 = 21$

- **Factorial ($n!$):**

    - $f(0) = 1$

    - $f(n+1) = (n+1) \cdot f(n)$

- **Summation ($\sum_{k=0}^{n} a_k$):**

    - Basis: $\sum_{k=0}^{0} a_k = a_0$

    - Recursive: $\sum_{k=0}^{n+1} a_k = \left(\sum_{k=0}^{n} a_k\right) + a_{n+1}$

## 2. The Fibonacci Sequence

The Fibonacci sequence models natural phenomena (like population growth) and is strictly defined via recursion:

- $f_0 = 0$

- $f_1 = 1$

- $f_n = f_{n-1} + f_{n-2}$ (for $n \geq 2$)

**Key Property (Proved via Strong Induction):**

For $n \geq 3$, $f_n > \alpha^{n-2}$, where $\alpha = \frac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}$ (the Golden Ratio).

- _Proof insight:_ The proof relies on the algebraic property that $\alpha^2 = \alpha + 1$. In the inductive step, substituting $\alpha^{k-1} = \alpha^{k-2} + \alpha^{k-3}$ perfectly matches the recursive sum $f_{k+1} = f_k + f_{k-1}$.

## 3. Euclidean Algorithm and Lamé's Theorem

The Euclidean Algorithm efficiently computes the greatest common divisor (gcd) using the principle that $\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)$.

**Lamé's Theorem:**

Let $a$ and $b$ be positive integers with $a \geq b$. The number of divisions used by the Euclidean algorithm to find $\gcd(a,b)$ is less than or equal to **five times the number of decimal digits in $b$**.

- _Complexity:_ As a direct consequence of Lamé's Theorem, the time complexity of the Euclidean algorithm is $O(\log b)$.

## 4. Recursively Defined Sets and Structures

Sets can be constructed using recursive definitions, which universally assume an **exclusion rule**: the set contains _only_ the elements specified by the basis step and those generated by the recursive step.

**Examples of Recursive Sets:**

- **Subset of Integers (Multiples of 3):**

    - Basis: $3 \in S$

    - Recursive: If $x \in S$ and $y \in S$, then $x + y \in S$.

- **Strings over an Alphabet ($\Sigma^*$):**

    - Basis: $\lambda \in \Sigma^*$ (where $\lambda$ is the empty string).

    - Recursive: If $w \in \Sigma^*$ and $x \in \Sigma$, then $wx \in \Sigma^*$.

- **Well-Formed Formulae in Propositional Logic:**

    - Basis: $\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{F}$, and propositional variables ($s$) are well-formed.

    - Recursive: If $E$ and $F$ are well-formed, then $(\neg E), (E \land F), (E \lor F), (E \rightarrow F), (E \leftrightarrow F)$ are well-formed.

**Recursive Operations on Strings:**

- **Concatenation ($w_1 \cdot w_2$):** Basis: $w \cdot \lambda = w$. Recursive: $w_1 \cdot (w_2x) = (w_1 \cdot w_2)x$.

- **Length ($l(w)$):** Basis: $l(\lambda) = 0$. Recursive: $l(wx) = l(w) + 1$.

## 5. Rooted and Full Binary Trees

**Rooted Trees:**

- Basis: A single vertex $r$ is a rooted tree.

- Recursive: Suppose $T_1, T_2, \dots, T_n$ are disjoint rooted trees with roots $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n$. A new graph formed by taking a root $r$ and adding edges to $r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n$ is also a rooted tree.

**Full Binary Trees:**

- Basis: A single vertex $r$ is a full binary tree.

- Recursive: If $T_1$ and $T_2$ are disjoint full binary trees, a new full binary tree $T_1 \cdot T_2$ is formed by connecting a root $r$ to the roots of $T_1$ and $T_2$.

- _Height $h(T)$:_ Basis: $h(T) = 0$. Recursive: $h(T) = 1 + \max(h(T_1), h(T_2))$.

- _Vertices $n(T)$:_ Basis: $n(T) = 1$. Recursive: $n(T) = 1 + n(T_1) + n(T_2)$.

## 6. Structural Induction

Structural induction is a proof technique used specifically for demonstrating that a property holds for all elements of a recursively defined set. Its validity stems from standard mathematical induction.

**Steps for Structural Induction:**

1. **Basis Step:** Show that the property holds for all elements specified in the basis step of the set's recursive definition.

2. **Recursive Step:** Show that if the property is true for the foundational elements used to construct new elements, it must also hold for the newly constructed elements.

**Example Theorem:** If $T$ is a full binary tree, then its number of vertices satisfies $n(T) \leq 2^{h(T)+1} - 1$.

- _Proof by Structural Induction:_

    - _Basis:_ For a single vertex tree, $n(T) = 1$ and $h(T) = 0$. $1 \leq 2^{0+1} - 1 = 1$. (True)

    - _Recursive:_ Assume $n(T_1) \leq 2^{h(T_1)+1} - 1$ and $n(T_2) \leq 2^{h(T_2)+1} - 1$.

        $n(T) = 1 + n(T_1) + n(T_2) \leq 1 + (2^{h(T_1)+1} - 1) + (2^{h(T_2)+1} - 1) \leq 2 \cdot \max(2^{h(T_1)+1}, 2^{h(T_2)+1}) - 1 = 2 \cdot 2^{h(T)} - 1 = 2^{h(T)+1} - 1$.

## 7. Generalized Induction

Generalized induction extends induction to sets beyond integers that possess a well-ordering property (e.g., $\mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}$ pairs or strings ordered lexicographically).

- **Lexicographic Ordering:** $(x_1, y_1)$ is less than or equal to $(x_2, y_2)$ if $x_1 < x_2$, or if $x_1 = x_2$ and $y_1 \leq y_2$.

## 8. Fractals (Visual Recursion)

Fractals are geometric shapes constructed using recursive patterns that repeat indefinitely across different scales and orientations.

- **Koch Curve:** A curve where order $n$ consists of four copies of order $n-1$.

- **Koch Snowflake:** Constructed from three Koch curves.

- **Sierpinski Triangle:** Formed by a confined recursion of extracting smaller triangles to create a geometric lattice.
